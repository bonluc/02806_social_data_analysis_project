Data Analysis  ·  New York City 2014–2025

Vision Zero:
Ten Years Later

In 2014, New York City promised to eliminate all traffic deaths within a decade. Did it keep that promise?

Introduction

Vision Zero is an initiative that originated in Sweden in 1997. Its core idea is that human well-being and safety come first. That means recognizing the fragility of humans and that errors are inevitable in traffic. Therefore, the infrastructure must be designed to prevent such errors from resulting in fatalities. Vision Zero is a policy that has now been adopted in multiple cities and countries globally. In the US, more than 40 cities have set a clear goal of eliminating traffic fatalities. New York City, being one of them, adopted this vision in 2014 with the goal of completely eliminating traffic deaths within 10 years.

In this analysis, we investigate whether that promise has been kept, how much progress has been made, and what could potentially be done to further reduce deaths based on data analysis.

The data used in this analysis comes from the NYC Open Data Motor Vehicle Collisions dataset, which covers all reported collisions in New York City from 2014 to the present day. The first thing we ask is: what is the current state of traffic collisions, injuries, and deaths in NYC and how has it changed since Vision Zero was adopted?

Finding 01

Fewer crashes — but are New Yorkers actually safer?

Fig. 1 — Total collisions, injuries and deaths per year from 2014 to 2025. Source: NYC Open Data.

We start with an overall assessment of collisions, injuries and deaths through the years. After the initiation of Vision Zero in 2014, we actually see the number of collisions increasing until 2018 with 10 %, the number of injuries increased by almost 20 % and number of deaths dropped almost 13%. Vision Zero is off to a rocky start but at least fatalities are going down. We then see a sharp drop in collisions in 2020 which is most likely explained by COVID-19 reducing commuting and street activity across the city. This is followed by an increase in death and injuries. Injuries do not reach the pre-COVID levels but deaths reach an all-time high, in some years more than 20% above pre-COVID levels. From 2023 to 2025 we then begin to see decline again in both injuries and deaths. So where do we stand in 2025?

In comparing 2014 to 2025, we see that collisions are down 58%, injuries are down just 3%, and deaths are down 13% and at an all-time low. While it's great collisions have been significantly reduced it's difficult to tell how much can be attributed to Vision Zero and how much can be attributed to COVID-19 permanently changing behaviours in traffic. In essence, Vision Zero's goal of zero deaths has not been met.

Fewer crashes are happening, but the ones that do are more likely to hurt or kill someone.

Because deaths and injuries have not fallen proportionally to collisions, the rate of injury and death per collision has actually increased since 2014. Are these fatalities potentially affected by external conditions such as weather?

Finding 02

Does weather affect fatality rates?

Fig. 2 — Weather conditions at time of all collisions vs fatal collisions only.

Before focusing on the collisions themselves, we wanted to investigate whether external conditions play a role in determining whether a collision turns fatal. Precipitation could make roads more slippery, cloud cover could reduce visibility, high wind speeds could affect the awareness of pedestrians and cyclists, and extreme temperatures could impair driver concentration. To test this, we matched each collision with hourly weather data and compared the distributions of precipitation, wind speed, cloud cover, and temperature across all collisions versus fatal collisions only.

Weather has no significant impact on fatality rates.

We found that the weather conditions during fatal collisions are not significantly different from the overall weather conditions across all collisions. This suggests that weather is not a major contributing factor to fatal collisions in NYC, and that other factors such as driver behavior, road design, and traffic enforcement are likely more important to address in order to reduce fatalities. Having ruled out weather, we next ask whether location plays a role, are some places in the city consistently more dangerous than others?

Finding 03

Where do fatal collisions occur?

Fig. 3 — Animated map of NYC traffic deaths 2014–2025. Each dot decays over 30 days. Use the play button or slider to explore.

The animated map shows every fatal collision in NYC from 2014 to 2025, with each death fading out over 30 days. The immediate takeaway is that deaths are not concentrated in one area, but occur across the entire city, in every borough, throughout the entire decade. To look for more structural patterns we turn to a heatmap that aggregates deaths by time of day.

Fig. 4 — Heatmap of NYC traffic deaths 2014–2025.

The heatmap reveals some hotspots of fatalities and we also see that the hotspots change depending on what time of day it is. Switching between midday, evening rush, and evening layers shows the hotspots shifting northward. But generally the deaths are still spread across the entire city. Using just the heatmap makes it hard to extract any meaningful insights about these locations. Even though the crashes are spread across the city, the locations may share some common characteristics". We therefore classify each collision by street type, dividing into intersection, mid-block, off-street, unknown.

Fig. 5 — Collisions vs Deaths by Location Type.

Intersections and mid-block sections are the most dangerous locations for fatal collisions.

The majority of collisions happen at intersections and off-street locations, while the majority of deaths happen at intersections and mid-block locations. This makes intuitive sense since mid-block sections lack the speed regulation found in off-street environments. The speed of the vehicles will not be as regulated by traffic lights, parking spots or crossing sections. This means the policy could focus on making mid-block sections safer by implementing traffic calming measures such as speed bumps, raised crosswalks, or chicanes to slow down traffic and reduce the severity of collisions in these areas. Having established where fatal collisions occur, we now turn to why they happen.

Finding 04

Why do fatal collisions happen?

Fig. 6 — Contributing factors — fatal collisions by year (2014–2025)

Interestingly we see that consistently across the years the top four contributing factors: unsafe speed, failure to yield right of way, traffic control disregarded and driver inattention are all related to driver behaviour and reflect poor decision making and risk assessment by the drivers. Another point to note is that unsafe speed in 2025 is the most common contributing factor in fatal collisions. This is interesting because among the initiatives of Vision Zero has been the introduction of more speed cameras, slow zones and lower speed limits. So while these measures might have had some effect in reducing the number of collisions, they do not seem to have had a significant effect on reducing the number of fatal collisions. This suggests that while road design and infrastructure improvements are important, they may not be sufficient to address all fatal collisions. To further reduce fatalities, one suggestion is to have a stronger focus on driver education, awareness campaigns, and enforcement of traffic laws to encourage safer driving behaviors, though this approach has already been employed. An alternative suggestion might be the design and engineering of the vehicles themselves.

Road design and infrastructure might not be enough to prevent fatal collisions if driver behaviour is not addressed.

If driver inattention and unsafe speed are the main contributing factors, then perhaps there is a role for technology to play in mitigating these risks. Many roads have their speed limit as part of their GPS location, integrating these with a vehicle's system to automatically adjust the speed by default could be a potential solution to reduce the number of fatal collisions caused by unsafe speed. The inattention and general disregard of traffic rules would require more advanced driving assistance systems that could detect potential hazardous situations and intervene when necessary to prevent a collision.

Conclusion

Mitigating traffic fatalities is a complex challenge requiring a multi-faceted approach

Ten years after Vision Zero was adopted, New York City has made progress but has not fully achieved the goal of eliminating traffic deaths. Collisions have fallen sharply, yet deaths and injuries remain stubbornly high. This exploration shows that driver behavior, not road design alone, is important to consider in mitigating fatal collisions. Unsafe speed, failure to yield, and disregard for traffic signals dominate fatal crashes. Addressing these requires stronger enforcement and technological interventions such as automatic speed regulation and advanced driver assistance systems. Intersections and mid-block areas are the most common locations for fatal collisions, giving a bounded scope for targeted interventions that can be tested in a relatively small number of locations and then scaled if successful.
