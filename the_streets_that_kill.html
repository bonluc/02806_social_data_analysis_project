The Streets That Kill.

Across fourteen years, two million collisions, and five boroughs, New York City keeps a quiet ledger of the people its streets killed. This is what that ledger looks like.

· NYC Open Data — Motor Vehicle Collisions
The scale

Two million crashes. Three thousand deaths.

Between 2012 and 2026, the NYPD recorded motor-vehicle collisions across New York City. Most ended in dented bumpers and exchanged insurance details. people were injured. never made it home.

The map of those fatal crashes is what we will end with. First, a few things the data has to say about when, where, and to whom.

The clock of danger

Most crashes happen at rush hour. Most deaths don't.

If you plot every crash by the hour of day, the curve looks exactly like the city's commute: a low trough at 4 a.m., a steep climb to a 4–6 p.m. peak, then a long evening fade. Crashes are a function of how many cars are on the road.

But fatal crashes follow a different rhythm. They flatten the morning rush hour and bulge in the deep night and small hours. Speeding, drinking, and emptier streets are deadlier than congestion.

By day of the week the pattern is more familiar: Saturday is the deadliest day, Wednesday the safest. Weekend evenings (Friday night, Saturday night) account for an outsized share of the fatalities.

The map of grief

The deaths are not evenly shared.

Brooklyn and Queens record the most fatal crashes by raw count — they are the largest boroughs and have the most cars. But the per-capita picture flips: Queens still leads, but the Bronx and Staten Island climb past Manhattan. Manhattan has the most crashes, but the slowest streets and the fewest deaths per resident.

The corridors

Ten streets. Hundreds of deaths.

A small handful of streets account for a disproportionate share of fatalities. The deadliest are the city's parkways, expressways, and long arterials — high speeds, multiple lanes, few signals. The Belt Parkway alone has killed more than fifty people in our window.

Who dies on these streets

Pedestrians take the brunt.

More than half of all traffic deaths in New York City are pedestrians — a far higher share than the national average. Cyclists, despite the rapid growth of cycling and bike infrastructure, account for around 8% of fatalities. The remainder are motorists, often killed at speed on highways and parkways.

The causes officers wrote down

Inattention. Speed. Failing to yield.

For roughly a third of fatal crashes, the contributing factor is left "Unspecified." For the rest, three causes dominate: distraction, unsafe speed, and failure to yield. Together they account for the majority of recorded fatalities — and all three are about driver behavior, not road geometry or weather.

About this story

All figures come from the NYC Open Data dataset Motor Vehicle Collisions — Crashes, covering July 2012 through April 2026. Fatal crashes are those in which at least one person was killed; "people killed" sums fatalities across all such crashes. Borough population figures are from the 2020 U.S. Census. Geographic filtering excludes records with missing or implausible coordinates. The interactive map shows every fatal crash with usable coordinates — no sampling.
